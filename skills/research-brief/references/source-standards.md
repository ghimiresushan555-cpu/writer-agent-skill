# Source standards

## Ranking

Prefer the highest rank that answers the question. Drop a rank only with a note explaining why.

| Rank | Type | Use for | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Primary data, filings, standards, transcripts, docs | Numbers, rules, definitions | Check the date and the universe covered |
| 2 | Named first-hand interview or direct observation | Experience, mechanism, what actually happens | One person is an anecdote; say so |
| 3 | Reputable secondary reporting that links to rank 1 | Access to paywalled primaries | Cite both the report and the primary |
| 4 | Reference works, textbooks, handbooks | Settled definitions and history | Often stale on anything fast-moving |
| 5 | Company blogs, press releases, marketing | What the company *claims* | Never neutral fact; always attribute |
| 6 | Social posts, forums, anonymous sources | Evidence a conversation exists; leads | Not a basis for a factual claim |
| 7 | AI summaries, aggregators, content farms | Finding rank 1–3 sources | Not a source. Ever. |

## The six verification checks

1. **Open it.** You must have actually loaded the source. A citation you didn't open is a fabrication waiting to happen.
2. **Date it.** Record publication date, not access date. Flag anything older than two years in a fast-moving field.
3. **Locate the exact figure.** Quote the sentence containing the number. If you can't find the sentence, you don't have the claim.
4. **Check the universe.** Who was measured, how many, over what period, in which country. Most bad citations are true statements about the wrong population.
5. **Follow it to origin.** If the source cites another source, go there. Relay chains mutate numbers.
6. **Test the falsifier.** Search for evidence the claim is wrong. If that search is easy, the claim is contested and must be framed as such.

## Failure modes to hunt

- **Statistic laundering** — a number cited only by people citing each other, with no study at the root.
- **The dead link** — verify URLs at draft time; sites rot within months.
- **Definition drift** — "users," "active," "revenue," "success" meaning different things in source and draft.
- **The unearned superlative** — "first," "only," "fastest" with no defined universe or date.
- **Range collapse** — "studies show" covering several studies that disagree.
- **The decontextualised quote** — accurate words, misleading framing. Reproduce enough of the quote that the meaning survives.
- **The confident percentage** — precise number, no methodology. Trace it or cut it.
- **Survey self-selection** — vendor surveys of their own customers. Usable as "the vendor reports," not as market truth.

## Recording a source

Minimum viable citation:

```
Author or org. "Title." Publisher, date. URL. Accessed 2026-11-04.
What it says: <the exact figure or quote>
What it doesn't say: <the limit — population, period, method>
```

The third line is what separates a brief from a bibliography. It's also the line that most often changes the article for the better.

## When nothing exists

Say it plainly: *"No public data on X. The closest is Y, which covers Z rather than X."*

Then offer the alternatives, in order of preference:

1. Interview someone who would know (name the kind of person and where to find them).
2. Run a small, disclosed, non-scientific poll of your own readers — and label it exactly that.
3. Write the piece about the absence: the fact that a $40bn industry has no public data on its own churn is a story.
4. Cut the claim and build the argument on what you do have.

Never bridge the gap with a plausible-sounding figure. That's the one error that ends a channel's credibility.
