# Six shapes, and when each fails

Pick the shape the evidence supports. Most drafts that stall are the wrong shape for their material.

## 1. Argument

**Engine:** a claim someone disagrees with, met by the strongest objection, resolved or held.

```
Hook: the claim, made concretely          (~10%)
What everyone assumes                     (~15%)
Why that's wrong — the mechanism          (~30%)
The strongest objection, at full strength (~20%)
Where the claim stops being true          (~15%)
What to do with this                      (~10%)
```

- **Use when** you have a point of view plus evidence.
- **Fails when** the objection is a strawman — readers stop trusting the rest of the piece instantly.
- **Hardest part** is the fourth block. Write the objection as if you believed it.

## 2. Mechanism teardown

**Engine:** sequence. The reader wants to know what step four actually is.

```
The outcome people see                  (~10%)
Step 1 — and why it looks trivial        (~20%)
Step 2 — where the cost hides            (~20%)
Step 3 — the decision point              (~25%)
Step 4 — the failure mode                (~15%)
What this means for your own version     (~10%)
```

- **Use when** you've done the thing and can describe the real sequence.
- **Fails when** you describe the idealized process instead of the one you lived. Name the workaround, the spreadsheet, the phone call.
- **Rule:** every step must contain something a reader couldn't have guessed.

## 3. Post-mortem

**Engine:** suspense in reverse. What we believed, what happened, why, and what it cost.

```
What we set out to do, and what we believed (~15%)
The moment it stopped working                (~15%)
Reason 1 (the avoidable one)                 (~20%)
Reason 2 (the structural one)                (~20%)
The number: cost, time, or loss              (~10%)
What we'd do differently, specifically       (~20%)
```

- **Use when** something failed and you're willing to be specific about the cost.
- **Fails when** the lesson is "communicate better" — that's a conclusion that fits every story, therefore none.
- **Rule:** the number is not optional. A post-mortem without a cost is a humblebrag.

## 4. Field report

**Engine:** observation. Specificity *is* the argument; resist the urge to generalize.

```
A scene, in present tense                   (~20%)
What surprised you                          (~25%)
What it confirms about the wider picture    (~25%)
What it complicates                         (~20%)
One thing to watch                          (~10%)
```

- **Use when** you were somewhere, or talked to someone doing the work.
- **Fails when** the last section over-claims: three site visits do not establish an industry trend. Say "in the three plants I visited."
- **Strength:** it's the hardest shape to fake, which is why it reads as credible.

## 5. Translation

**Engine:** two vocabularies that don't map, and the cost of the gap.

```
Term A as one group uses it                 (~20%)
Term B as the other group uses it           (~20%)
Why the gap exists                          (~20%)
Where it costs money, in a concrete case    (~25%)
How to tell which one you're in a meeting   (~15%)
```

- **Use when** two functions, industries, or generations talk past each other.
- **Fails when** it becomes a glossary. Every definition needs a consequence attached.

## 6. Annotated example

**Engine:** the real artifact, with commentary in the margins.

```
What this document is and why it matters    (~15%)
The artifact, in chunks                     (~50%)
Commentary on each chunk                    (~25%)
What to copy and what to change             (~10%)
```

- **Use when** the value is in the object itself — a contract clause, an email, a spreadsheet, a pricing page.
- **Fails when** the artifact is confidential and gets paraphrased into vagueness. If it can't be shown, choose another shape.
- **Rule:** get permission before publishing a real document, and say what you changed.

## Choosing in ten seconds

| If the brief's strongest material is... | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| A surprising claim plus counterevidence | Argument |
| Your own hard-won process | Mechanism |
| A failure with a number attached | Post-mortem |
| Time spent somewhere with your own eyes | Field report |
| Two groups misunderstanding each other | Translation |
| A document that speaks for itself | Annotated example |

## Length budgeting

Assign word counts per block *before* writing, and hold them. The universal failure is a 350-word opening and a 60-word ending. If a block overruns by 50%, cut it or move the overflow into the next block — don't steal from the ending, which is the part readers remember.
